Many developers always have the vague idea and desire of what they want to build, but do not have the time or energy or determinations to build them out. The thing is, you usually have all those. What you don't have is clear understanding of what exactly you want to build and how to break it down.

I made about 1600 commits to my github this last year.

## What to Build

First, build something that you would use. As Cliche as that sounds, this is the best way to get started.
I've tattled with audio player that directly loads and plays some of my favorite music by putting the files up in the github. As audio player, the functionality is primitive and amatuerish. But for my use case, of not having to carry a DAP or install audio player and carry my flac files whenever I work on different machines, this actually does the job. The apparent downsides (memory inefficiency, non-general use case, aesthetically and UX wise there are many more generally appealing alternatives) do not really matter, because this solves the problem I had that made my workflow and my day a little more cumbersome. It scratches that itch for me. and isn't this the heart of programming? to use code as a tool for your problems? the problems you solve maybe complicated, but the value you bring for yourself (or anyone) can be much simpler and smaller. Not everything you do should be for general purposes, because the solutions we architect are strictly and tightly bound to the contexts in which we face.
Another example here is my static audio spectrum analyzer, Spektra. I have a hobby in audio, and usually after purchasing Lossless audio online in format of FLAC files, I would verify is these actually have full dynamic range of signals using great tools like spek.cc. However, this requires install to your machine. I remember when I was using three different OS, having to transfer files to a different machine to avoid installing spek on my strict productivity setup. Sure I can install this and it is very light weight, amazing software, but I would only download music on that machine once in a blue moon. So I decided I can just build a static webapp that runs FFT for spectrum analysis. It's not the fastest (I had to actually write FFT myself in Rust and compile it to WASM for fast enough processing), but for my usecase of occasionally checking if an audio file has full range of signals, this works perfectly. It doesn't require install, doesn't require configuration, just drag and drop and see the metadata and spectogram.

Second, once you practice the process of finding out an "itch" that you have and designing and writing code to scratch that itch, do that for people around you. The very process of understanding someone else's perspective and need is precisely how you get better at requirement gathering. You also learn not to overengineer your solutions. Most importantly if you haven't worked directly with a client before, you will learn fast that people (much like yourself if you've gone through the first phase) are actually not good in stating what they want. The user generally has a vague feeling about the ultimate solution they want, and requirement gathering is the process of translating this into tacklable problems at each level and piecing them together. For me, building a portfolio website for my friends, or a web scraper for my family, audio editing tool, even something as simple as web app that gives you quick calculation of Fahrenheit to Celcius changes, were this phase. The technical problems you tackle will vary in difficulty, but the more you do this the more you realize that the level of effort and technical difficulty of a problem you solve just does not map linearly to the value you can bring to the people.

Thirdly, once you have scratched enough itches, the next step is to build something that you want, not just need. Again, none of these have to be big. For me, I built a visualizer for financial models in SPA using typescript. Why? because while matplotlib and python are the default language for these financial modeling, the issue is that people who are non-technical (and I mean, do not have great command over even python because they didn't have the exposure or background) would often struggle to understand how to work each parts or fix exactly what they need for their use case. So I built the visualizer on SPA with sliders for parameters and common graphs, so that anyone like some of my friends back in college who wanted to visualize those models but couldn't quite figure out how to change the python code for the aesthetics they wanted or even the value they wanted, to be able to to them without hessle.
Another project was Rust based CLI/TUI audio player. Of course there are great tools out there that already does this, for me personally I use cmus and kew, but I wanted to get a better understanding of the inner workings of audio player, CLI/TUI, Rust language, and most importantly, have an aesthetic that is catering to me and only me: the n=1 market.

Fourth, after you've gotten sufficient experience doing these smaller but useful and valuable items, you can move onto shadowing or cloning. Not following the youtube tutorial on "clone X website in Y Hours" videos, but applying what you have been doing on a smaller scale to a larger scale, using the tools that they are using. For me, MiniMeet (real time video chat with fullstack Typescript and WebSocket, WebRTC, gRPC for communcation) and HistoFlow (Cancer call classification application with OpenSeadragon for Tiling, Kotling + Spring Boot for backend, Python + FastAPI for ML Services, and PostgreSQL for DB and MiniO / S3 for storage) have been these apps. I think most people when they think of portfolio projects, think this should be the bar. The things is, while this can be useful at demonstrating your skills, it doesn't really bring value -> it doesn't solve a real problem that you have (except if that problem is I need a job and this will help me get interviews, then sure). It doesn't solve any inconveniences that others have (you can't see the immediate impact of your work and value delivered). It isn't something that other people will use because what you build will be less efficient in many ways to existing solutions especially if you are building more of POC/MVP app for skill developments. Most importantly, It generally would not be what you want to build. It's likely what the recruiters and hiring managers would be looking out for to see if it demonstrates specific skillsets, but unless your goal was to build that system, it's just not that fulfilling.

Lastly, you can actually build that amorphous idea that just floats in the back of your mind. For me, this is visualization and interactive app that will help people understand complex concepts like Heidegger's Hermeneutic Circle, of Benjamin's Arcade Project, and application of Phenomenology to Algorithmic Problem Solving, game theory and statistics for game's balance patches. I think this is the level which the people want their projects to be at, or thinks it should be at to show your capabilities. But in my opinion, this is the level most people don't get to not due to the lack of ability but due to lack of interests and methodology in streamlining their ideas into concrete.

Beyond this, you maybe be fortunate enough to solve a truly novel and in demand problems that will make you the next billionaire. But in reality, the people who do that are few not because the effort or the vision lacks, but rather because the nature of those solutions are rarely grand schemes that were designed to be what they are today, but contingent responses to contextual nature of problems that were met with lucky and well deserved welcome of the people as it fulfilled their needs.

## How to build

I really think every personal project should be a testing ground for your system design skills. For me, arguably the most time I spend on a project especially the smaller ones, is ADRs and System desing. to explicitly compare my ideas and document the process of my problem breakdown. The actual implementation is much easier and prompter when the steps and choices are clear, especially small toy projects that we like to build.

Also, always finish your projects, even if they are not great. even if it is just bare POC. Finishing a side project is actually the key to continuously building them.

Don't think you have to solve novel problems, but show how you solved them. Write a blog post or just analysis of your workflow, a developement log of sorts.
