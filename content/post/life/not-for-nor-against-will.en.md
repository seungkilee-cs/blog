# Not For Nor Against Will: A New Model of Action

Human action has been a perennial philosophical puzzle, oscillating between the forces of desire and discipline. For centuries, thinkers have sought to explain what drives us to act—whether it is the pull of our wants, the push of our rationality, or some synthesis of the two. Yet, these models often fail to address the deeper arbitrariness of desire itself and the limits of willpower. This article critically examines traditional and modern theories of action before proposing a new model: one that neither submits to nor resists desire but transcends it entirely through decision and capacity.

---

## Traditional Models of Desire and Action

### Aristotelian and Kantian Foundations

Aristotle’s teleological ethics placed desire at the heart of human action. For Aristotle, every action aims at some perceived good, with reason guiding desire toward virtuous ends. Desire is not an obstacle but a natural force that must be harmonized with rational deliberation to achieve _eudaimonia_ (flourishing). Yet, this harmony presupposes that desires can be shaped or educated—a premise that falters when confronted with desires that resist rational alignment.

Kant, by contrast, sought to subordinate desire entirely to reason. His moral philosophy hinges on the autonomy of the will, which he defined as the capacity to act according to self-imposed laws rather than external inclinations. For Kant, moral action arises from adherence to the categorical imperative: “Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law.” While Kant’s framework elevates reason above desire, it paradoxically relies on a form of volitional spontaneity—an inner principle that inclines the will toward rational duty. This reliance exposes a tension: if reason must still “move” the will, then it cannot fully escape the gravitational pull of inclination.

### Enlightenment Philosophers and Their Limitations

The Enlightenment’s faith in reason as humanity’s guiding star permeated its theories of action. Thinkers like Descartes and Spinoza emphasized the power of rationality to master passions and align actions with higher truths. Spinoza’s _conatus_—the striving for self-preservation—suggested that human beings could achieve freedom by understanding their desires within the deterministic order of nature. However, this deterministic view reduces freedom to mere acquiescence to necessity, leaving little room for genuine agency.

Similarly, Hume’s empiricism reduced desire to a mechanistic force: “Reason is, and ought only to be, the slave of the passions.” While Hume acknowledged reason’s role in identifying means to ends, he denied its capacity to determine ends themselves. This relegation of reason underscores a recurring problem: if desires are arbitrary and beyond rational control, how can they serve as a stable foundation for action?

### Schopenhauer’s Radical Critique

Schopenhauer shattered Enlightenment optimism with his profound insight: _“Man can do what he wills, but not will what he wills.”_ Unlike his predecessors, Schopenhauer recognized that human beings are not masters of their own desires. The will operates as an irrational metaphysical force—a blind striving that underpins all existence. While we may act upon our desires, we cannot choose them; they arise unbidden from our character and unconscious drives.

This insight exposes the fragility of traditional models: if desire itself is arbitrary and uncontrollable, then any framework reliant on mastering or harmonizing it is inherently flawed. Schopenhauer’s critique sets the stage for modern theories of action but also highlights their ultimate futility.

## Modern Models of Desire and Action

### Lacanian Desire as Structural Lack

Jacques Lacan redefined desire as an expression of lack—a void at the core of human subjectivity. For Lacan, desire arises not from need but from an unattainable object (_objet petit a_) mediated by language and social structures. Action becomes a perpetual attempt to fill this void, yet fulfillment remains forever deferred. This model captures the complexity of human motivation but traps agency within an endless cycle of pursuit and frustration.

### Deleuzian-Guattarian Desiring-Machines

Deleuze and Guattari rejected Lacan’s emphasis on lack, instead framing desire as productive rather than deficient. In their view, desire generates connections and assemblages within broader systems they call “desiring-machines.” Action arises not from individual lack but from collective flows that produce new realities. While this model celebrates creativity and dynamism, it sacrifices individual agency on the altar of impersonal systems.

### Comparing Lacanian and Deleuzian Models

Both Lacanian and Deleuzian frameworks grapple with the uncontrollability of desire but arrive at different conclusions: Lacan sees it as an insatiable void; Deleuze sees it as an overflowing force. Yet both fail to provide actionable solutions for individuals seeking autonomy in their daily lives.

---

## Critique of Structuralist and Post-Structuralist Models

### The Unanswerable Root of Desire

Structuralist (Lacan) and post-structuralist (Deleuze) models sidestep a fundamental problem: the origin of desire itself remains unknowable and uncontrollable. This mirrors Aristotle’s “first cause” problem—an infinite regress that leaves us with no actionable starting point. If desire cannot be consistently influenced or understood, these models offer little practical guidance for shaping action.

### Bias Against Human Agency

The Frankfurt School’s critique of Enlightenment rationalism resonates here: thinkers like Adorno and Horkheimer exposed how reason often devolves into domination rather than liberation. Similarly, structuralist models reduce humans to passive products of linguistic or systemic forces—a bitter recoil against reductionist rationalism that culminates in existential despair (_Geworfenheit_). Yet this pessimism overlooks moments where individuals transcend deterministic cycles through deliberate choice.

---

## Problems with Existing Drivers of Action

### Motivation as Driver

Motivation is inherently unstable—it fluctuates based on mood, environment, and unconscious impulses. Acting solely on what one “feels like” doing reduces agency to whimsy—a reactive mode that leaves individuals at the mercy of fleeting desires.

### Discipline as Driver

Discipline offers a more reliable alternative by prioritizing long-term goals over short-term gratification. However, it often frames action as resistance against lack—fighting inertia or unwillingness through sheer force of will. This adversarial approach is unsustainable; like fighting hunger through deprivation alone, it risks eventual collapse.

---

## A New Model: Neither For Nor Against Wills

### Decoupling Action from Desire

This new model proposes a radical departure: eliminate will—whether for or against—from the equation entirely. Instead:

1. **Act Based on Decision**: Choose actions deliberately without regard for how you feel about them.
2. **Operate Within Capacity**: Act only within what you are physically or mentally capable of doing.

For example: going to the gym at 5:30 AM should neither be motivated by a desire for fitness nor resisted due to fatigue or dislike for mornings. Instead, you go because you decided to go—and because you are physically able.

### Short-Term Action as Transformative Feedback

Humans are both products _and_ functions of their actions—a cyclical relationship where what we do shapes who we become. By removing desire from decision-making in the short term, we create conditions for long-term transformation: repeated actions rewire habits and dispositions over time.

---

## Conclusion

The traditional reliance on motivation or discipline has proven inadequate in addressing human agency within deterministic systems of desire. By decoupling action from will entirely—neither acting _for_ nor _against_ one’s desires—the proposed model offers a pragmatic framework for overcoming inertia while fostering long-term transformation through deliberate decision-making.

This is not resignation but liberation: freedom not from determinism but within it—a choice made possible precisely because it transcends want altogether.
